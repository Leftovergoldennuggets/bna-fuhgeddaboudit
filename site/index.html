The fatal crashes

-- crashes on record involved a death. According to the reports Waymo filed and to police accounts, in each one the Waymo was stopped or slowing and was hit as a result of another road user's actions; no Waymo has been found at fault.

  • San Francisco, January 2025. An empty Waymo was stopped in traffic when a speeding driver plowed into a line of cars, pushing them into the Waymo. One person and a dog were killed; the speeding driver was charged.
  • Tempe, Ariz., September 2025. An empty Waymo was slowing at 8 mph to turn right when a motorcycle hit it from behind. The rider fell and was run over by a passing car whose driver fled; police later sought a felony charge against that driver.
  • Dallas, August 2026. An empty Waymo had slowed to about 5 mph for a pedestrian in the road when an SUV in the opposite lanes struck the pedestrian, who was thrown into the Waymo's rear. Dallas police had not publicly assigned fault in the latest reports.

Sources: Waymo's reports to NHTSA (report IDs 30270-9724, -11713, -16196); San Francisco, Tempe and Dallas police via CBS SF, azfamily, NBC DFW and Fox 4.

Are Waymos Getting Safer — and Are They Safer Than People?

Crash counts can't answer that. Crashes per mile can.

Waymo's Driverless Miles

Cumulative rider-only miles. Filled points are Waymo's quarterly data releases, added automatically; hollow points are milestones from Waymo announcements and research papers. Hover for each source.

Waymo now drives about -- million driverless miles a quarter, so crash counts rise even if each mile gets safer. Dividing crashes by miles gives a rate — and the rate for all reported crashes appears to halve in mid-2025.

That drop is mostly a paperwork change. On June 16, 2025, new federal rules stopped requiring reports for many minor crashes in which the self-driving car was hit and damage stayed under $1,000. The rate of crashes in which someone was injured — which must still be reported — barely moved.

Compared With Human Drivers

Waymo compares its crash rates with human drivers on the same kinds of roads in the same cities, using a method published in a peer-reviewed journal. Across -- driverless miles through --, its cars were in far fewer crashes of every severity:

Bars show the 95% confidence interval; the dot is Waymo's estimate. "Expected for human drivers" is how many crashes human drivers would have had over the same miles. Source: Waymo Safety Impact Data Hub (CSV3, "dynamic" benchmark), read automatically each quarter.

Read with care. This is Waymo's analysis of Waymo. The method has been peer-reviewed (Kusano et al., 2025), but human benchmarks can't perfectly match where, when and how Waymo drives. The rarest outcomes rest on very few crashes: the serious-injury and fatal comparisons are based on -- and -- Waymo crashes, hence the wide ranges.

An independent check points the same way, less dramatically. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (July 2026) analyzed about 50 million Waymo miles from 2021–2024 and found 68% fewer police-reportable crashes and 81% fewer injury crashes per mile than human drivers in the same cities — but only 35% fewer in San Francisco, and slightly more in Austin, where its sample was small.

When Do Crashes Happen?

Crashes cluster in the afternoon and evening and on Fridays and Saturdays. Part of that is busier roads; part is simply when Waymo's cars are out carrying riders. Waymo doesn't publish miles by hour, so these patterns show when crashes happen — not when driving is riskier.
