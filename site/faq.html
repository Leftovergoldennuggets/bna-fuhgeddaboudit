Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to common questions about this project and the data behind it. For a more detailed technical discussion, see our Methodology page.

Where does this data come from?

Two sources: NHTSA (the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration) and Waymo's Safety Impact Data Hub.

NHTSA requires all autonomous vehicle companies to report crashes resulting in injury, airbag deployment, or property damage exceeding $1,000 through their Standing General Order program. That federal record is the backbone of this site: every reported crash starts there. Waymo's quarterly data release then enriches matched crashes with exact dates, street addresses, and crash-type classifications. Because Waymo's release lags the federal data by a few months, the newest crashes appear here with city-level locations and month-level dates until the next quarterly drop fills in the details.

The dataset covers -- through --. However, the data is heavily concentrated in 2023–2025, reflecting Waymo's expansion from early commercial operations to large-scale service.

In the earliest years of the dataset (2020–2022), only a handful of driverless crashes were reported — Waymo's fleet was small and still operating with safety drivers in many cases. The data becomes substantial starting in 2023 when Waymo launched fully public, rider-only service in San Francisco, accelerates through 2024 and 2025 (Los Angeles, Austin, Atlanta), and broadens again in 2026 as service opened in Miami, Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, Orlando, and Nashville.

The headline numbers on this site cover fully driverless operation — no human behind the wheel. Crashes that happened during supervised testing with a safety driver (-- on record) are excluded from the totals but can be viewed in the Explore section with the "Include supervised-testing crashes" toggle.

How often is this website updated?

Automatically, every week. Our pipeline re-downloads the federal NHTSA data (which is refreshed roughly monthly) and checks for new Waymo quarterly releases (published around March, June, September, and December) every Monday. New crashes typically appear here within days of NHTSA publishing them — first as preliminary records with city-level locations, then with full detail once Waymo's quarterly release catches up.

Did the reporting rules change in 2025?

Yes. On June 16, 2025, NHTSA's Third Amended Standing General Order took effect, changing the crash reporting requirements for autonomous vehicle companies. Key changes include:

  • A property damage threshold was added — companies no longer need to report very minor property-damage-only incidents below a certain level
  • Reporting deadlines were extended from one day to five days for the most serious crashes
  • Duplicate reporting by multiple companies involved in the same crash was eliminated

This means data from before and after June 2025 isn't perfectly comparable — the earlier data includes some very minor incidents that wouldn't meet the new reporting threshold. Our analysis covers both periods and notes this distinction in the Methodology page.

What counts as a "crash"?

Under NHTSA's Standing General Order, companies must report any incident involving an autonomous vehicle that results in injury, airbag deployment, or property damage exceeding $1,000. Before June 2025, the threshold was even lower — any property damage at all was reportable, which included very minor incidents like parking lot bumps.

This relatively low reporting threshold means the crash numbers look high, but the vast majority of reported incidents are minor property-damage-only events with no injuries.

How does Waymo compare to human drivers?

Waymo publishes crash rate comparisons on its Safety Impact Data Hub. Important caveats:

  • These comparisons were conducted by Waymo researchers, though the methodology has been peer-reviewed by independent academics
  • The human benchmarks are adjusted to reflect the specific areas where Waymo operates (urban surface streets), using a "dynamic benchmarking" method. This means the comparison isn't against the average American driver everywhere — it's against human drivers in similar conditions
  • The methodology adheres to the RAVE checklist (Retrospective Automated Vehicle Evaluation), an industry standard developed jointly by researchers, academics, and insurance organizations including the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety

We include these figures for context but note that they are Waymo's own analysis of their own performance.

    What are the most common crash types?

    The most common crash type is rear-end collisions — where another vehicle hits the back of the Waymo. This is also the most common crash type for human drivers. Other frequent types include side-impact collisions and intersection crashes.

    Crashes involving pedestrians, cyclists, or motorcyclists are less common but receive special attention because of the higher potential for injury. You can filter by these categories in the Explore section.

    Does crash type mean Waymo was at fault?

    No. Crash types (e.g., "rear-end collision") describe the physical dynamics of the collision — where contact occurred between vehicles. They do not indicate legal fault. NHTSA reports do not assign fault or determine responsibility. In most crashes in this dataset, the Waymo vehicle was stationary at the time of contact.

    Why do most crashes happen during rush hour?

    About --% of crashes occur during rush hours (7–9 AM and 5–7 PM). This isn't surprising — these are the times when roads are busiest and the most vehicles (both human-driven and autonomous) are on the road. More traffic means more opportunities for collisions.

    Why are there so many crashes in parking lots?

    About --% of reported crashes occur in parking areas. This is consistent with industry research showing that parking lots account for a significant share of all vehicle collisions — the tight spaces, frequent reversing, and pedestrian traffic create more opportunities for minor contact.

    Nearly all parking lot crashes in the dataset resulted in property damage only, with no injuries reported.

    Why does this site count more crashes than Waymo's own data hub?

    Two reasons. First, freshness: Waymo's Safety Impact Data Hub is updated quarterly and lags a few months behind, while we ingest the federal NHTSA record weekly — so the most recent crashes appear here before they appear in Waymo's own dataset.

    Second, independence: we count from the federal record rather than from Waymo's curated release. In practice the two agree almost perfectly for the periods both cover — Waymo's hub has matched the federal driverless record nearly one-for-one — which is itself a useful transparency check, and we re-verify it on every update.

    Separately, NHTSA's data also contains -- Waymo crashes from supervised testing (a human safety driver behind the wheel), which Waymo's rider-only hub excludes by design. We keep those out of the headline numbers too, but unlike before, they're visible in the Explore section rather than silently dropped.

    Has Waymo been investigated or recalled?

    Yes — a few times, and it's useful context that crash counts alone don't capture:

    • School bus recall (December 2025). Waymo issued a software recall covering 3,000+ vehicles after federal regulators investigated its vehicles illegally passing stopped school buses with their stop arms extended. Austin's school district subsequently reported continued violations after the recall.
    • School-zone investigation (January 2026). NHTSA opened a preliminary investigation (PE26001) after a Waymo struck a child who ran into the street from behind a double-parked vehicle near a Santa Monica elementary school. The vehicle braked from 17 mph to under 6 mph before contact; the child's injuries were minor.
    • Floodwater recall (May 2026). Waymo recalled 3,791 robotaxis (fixed via software update) after an empty vehicle drove into a flooded San Antonio road and was swept into a creek. San Antonio service was paused for roughly two weeks.

    Recalls for autonomous fleets typically mean a software update pushed to all vehicles, not a physical return. We mention these because a fair safety picture includes regulatory scrutiny, not just crash statistics.
