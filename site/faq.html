Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to common questions about this project and the data behind it. For a more detailed technical discussion, see our Methodology page.

Where does this data come from?

Two public sources: crash reports Waymo files with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) under its Standing General Order, and Waymo's Safety Impact Data Hub.

The federal reports are the backbone: every crash on this site starts there. Waymo's quarterly data release then adds exact dates, street locations and crash-type classifications, plus the miles Waymo has driven. Because Waymo's release lags the federal data by a few months, the newest crashes appear with month-level dates and approximate locations until the next release fills them in. Details are on the methodology page.

What counts as a "crash"?

Whatever Waymo is required to report. Since June 16, 2025, that means any crash involving a death, a hospital-treated injury, an airbag deployment, a pedestrian or cyclist, or a towed vehicle; plus any other crash where damage is expected to exceed $1,000 or where the Waymo hit another vehicle or object. Before that date, any crash with any property damage counted — including very light bumps.

So the dataset is full of minor incidents: most involve no injury at all. That's a strength (it's far more complete than police data for human drivers) and a reason to read raw counts carefully.

Did the reporting rules change?

Yes. On June 16, 2025, NHTSA's Third Amended Standing General Order took effect. It stopped requiring reports for many minor crashes (roughly: the self-driving car was hit and damage stayed under $1,000), extended the deadline for the most serious reports from one day to five, and stopped requiring every company involved in the same crash to file.

The effect is visible in the data: Waymo's rate of all reported crashes per mile fell sharply across its cities at the same time, while its rate of injury crashes — reportable under both rules — barely changed. Comparisons across June 2025 are not like-for-like.

Does this mean Waymo wasn't at fault?

Not necessarily. Federal reports don't assign fault, and neither does this site. A Waymo stopped in the middle of an intersection, or braking unexpectedly, can contribute to a crash it doesn't physically cause. The facts here describe what happened, not who was responsible.

Are Waymos getting safer over time?

Hard to say yet. Waymo's rate of all reported crashes per mile fell by almost half in mid-2025 — but that coincides exactly with the reporting-rule change and appears across its cities at once, so much of it reflects paperwork rather than driving. Its rate of injury crashes, which is unaffected by the change, has stayed roughly flat at well under one per million miles. See the charts on the home page.

  • It's Waymo's own analysis, though the method has been peer-reviewed (Kusano et al., 2025).
  • Human benchmarks can't perfectly match where, when and how Waymo drives.
  • The most severe outcomes rest on few crashes, so their ranges are wide — the home page shows the uncertainty for each.

An independent study by the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (July 2026), covering about 50 million miles from 2021–2024, found 68% fewer police-reportable crashes and 81% fewer injury crashes per mile than human drivers — with a much smaller gap in San Francisco (35% fewer) and slightly more crashes in Austin, where its sample was small.

      When do most crashes happen?

      Crashes peak in the afternoon and evening and on Fridays and Saturdays; --% happen during rush hours (7–10 a.m. and 5–8 p.m.). That mostly reflects when roads are busiest and when Waymo's cars are out carrying riders. Waymo doesn't publish miles by hour, so the timing alone can't tell you whether some hours are riskier.

      Why does this site count more crashes than Waymo's own data hub?

      Freshness: Waymo's hub is updated quarterly and lags a few months, while this site reads the federal record weekly, so recent crashes appear here first. For the periods both cover, the two agree almost one-for-one — a useful check on both. NHTSA's data also contains -- Waymo crashes from supervised testing, which Waymo's hub leaves out by design and which we keep out of the headline numbers too.

      Has Waymo been investigated or recalled?

      Yes. Crash counts alone don't capture that kind of scrutiny, so here is the record as of October 2026. A "recall" for a self-driving fleet usually means a software update sent to every car, not a trip to the shop.

      • School buses (2025–26). After NHTSA opened an investigation (PE25013, October 2025) into Waymos passing stopped school buses, Waymo recalled about 3,000 vehicles in December 2025. Austin's school district reported further violations after the fix, and the National Transportation Safety Board opened its own investigation in January 2026.
      • Child struck near a school (January 2026). NHTSA (PE26001) and the NTSB investigated after a Waymo hit a child who ran into the street from behind an SUV near a Santa Monica elementary school. The car braked from 17 mph to under 6 mph; the child's injuries were minor.
      • Floodwater (April–May 2026). After an empty Waymo drove into a flooded San Antonio road and was swept into a creek, Waymo recalled about 3,800 vehicles. San Antonio service was paused from April until mid-September 2026.
      • Freeway work zones (June 2026). Waymo recalled 3,871 vehicles after its cars entered closed freeway construction zones 13 times in Phoenix and the Bay Area; no one was hurt. Freeway service was restricted while a fix was developed.
      • Earlier. Three smaller software recalls in 2024–2025, including one after a Waymo hit a utility pole.
      Can I download the data?

      Yes. Download every crash as a CSV file (rebuilt weekly; columns are explained in the data dictionary). The original sources are public too: NHTSA's Standing General Order data and the Waymo Safety Impact Data Hub. The code that builds this site is open source.
